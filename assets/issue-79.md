<comment>
Thank you for the question and for your participation in the contest.

To clarify, the evaluation is proceeding with the first option: the schedules generated by your solver are verified and scored using an abstract cost-model evaluator, not executed on physical hardware. 

As noted in the PROBLEM.md (under "Modeling Simplification for Experts"), the contest uses a highly simplified, abstract hardware model designed specifically to test graph scheduling logic, rather than low-level cycle counting or hardware-specific architecture. Nobody builds hardware this simple in real life.

Regarding your concern about the evaluator missing "special cases for fusion": the evaluation is strictly bound to the mathematical cost model defined in the PROBLEM.md specification. The only variables a schedule can manipulate are the explicit parameters provided in the output JSON. Participants should not make assumptions about internal hardware behaviors, pipeline optimizations, or compute/memory interactions that are not explicitly defined in the spec. If an optimization relies on imagined hardware details outside of the provided roofline model, it is not a valid strategy for this contest. 

However, because we have a diverse audience - ranging from those who have never worked with a compiler to deep systems experience or optimization expertise (yes, these are all different roles each specializes in a very different yet technically deep field) - we recognize that the abstract model may leave a few specific edge cases open to minor interpretation. 

To ensure the evaluation is as fair and transparent as possible, our validation process handles these interpretation differences carefully:

   1. Hard Fails: Absolute violations of the physical constraints defined in the spec (e.g., your binary  crashes, times out, or the schedule requires a working set that mathematically exceeds the fast_memory_capacity).
   2. Soft Warnings & Normalization: For the potential edge cases where the spec allows for slightly divergent interpretations of the cost model, the evaluator flags these as warnings rather than immediate disqualifications. 

We run extensive ablation studies on these warnings across the entire submission pool. This allows us to analyze how different, mathematically defensible interpretations impact the scoreboard. Crucially, this is exactly why the contest uses the relative normalization system detailed in the "Evaluation and Scoring" section of the README.md.

This scoring mechanism was designed specifically to absorb minor interpretation differences and ensure teams are scored fairly against the relative performance of their peers, rather than against a rigid, hidden "source of truth."

Finally, a major component of our evaluation is self-cohesiveness and stability. Even if a solver's specific latency calculation differs slightly from our baseline evaluator due to a valid interpretation difference, we look for that cost model to be consistently applied across the diverse set of 24 withheld benchmarks. Solvers that demonstrate a stable, self-consistent understanding of their own scheduling strategy perform exceptionally well under the relative normalization system.
</comment>
